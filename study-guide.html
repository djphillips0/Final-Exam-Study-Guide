Where to spend your time

  • Psychiatry (groups 1–2) is a quarter of the exam and is all new material since Exam 5. Know the boundaries between look-alike disorders (mood vs. psychotic, the personality clusters, the anxiety disorders), and know first-line treatment and safety steps.
  • Localization is still the backbone of the cumulative half. Groups 3–5 together are about 24 questions. Practice working out level, side and tract from an exam, and vessel from a deficit or image.
  • Expect several items on one idea from different angles. Recurring concepts: eye movement pathways (internuclear control and the pupil), the posterior circulation, a hyperkinetic movement disorder, primary headache with autonomic features, demyelination, and memory circuitry.
  • Images matter. Expect CT and MRI (bleeds, infarcts, tumors, spinal cord), gross specimens and histology. Know what each modality shows best.
  • None of the block exam questions are reused, but the concepts behind them are. If you missed a concept on Exams 1–5, it is worth reviewing.

1. Psychiatric diagnosis and mental status (9 questions)

Most of these items give you a patient and ask for the disorder, or give you the disorder and ask what else you would see on mental status exam. The skill is finding the one feature that separates look-alikes: timing, duration, level of function, or whether symptoms persist outside a mood episode.

Mood and psychotic disorders: timing is the key

Psychosis can appear in mood disorders, primary psychotic disorders and the overlap between them. Ask two questions: are there mood episodes, and do psychotic symptoms ever occur when mood is normal?

Disorder Mood episodes Psychosis Duration and function cues
Bipolar I Mania (≥ 7 days, or any length if hospitalized) Only during mood episodes, if present Mania impairs function or needs hospitalization
Bipolar II Hypomania (≥ 4 days) plus major depression Never in hypomania Change is observable by others but function is preserved
Major depression with psychotic features Depression Only during the episode Mood-congruent themes (guilt, ruin) are common
Persistent depressive disorder Chronic low mood ≥ 2 years No Milder, long-standing
Brief psychotic disorder None required Yes 1 day to < 1 month, full return to baseline
Schizophreniform None required Yes 1 to < 6 months
Schizophrenia Brief or absent relative to psychosis Yes ≥ 6 months with functional decline
Schizoaffective Major mood episodes for most of the illness Yes, including ≥ 2 weeks with normal mood Psychosis outlasts the mood episode
Delusional disorder Not prominent Delusions only, function largely preserved ≥ 1 month

Hypomania vs. mania. Both share elevated or irritable mood with DIGFAST features (distractibility, impulsivity, grandiosity, flight of ideas, increased activity, decreased need for sleep, talkativeness). Hypomania is shorter, does not impair function and never includes psychosis. Depression produces the opposite mental status findings: psychomotor slowing, latency of speech, poverty of content.

Same concept from different angles. Catatonia is a syndrome, not a diagnosis. It occurs with mood disorders, schizophrenia and medical causes (encephalitis, lupus, drug toxicity). Know its motor signs by name, and be able to tell its signs apart from findings in other disorders that sound similar.

Neurodevelopmental, reproductive-related and substance-related presentations

  • Autism spectrum disorder needs persistent deficits in social communication (back-and-forth conversation, nonverbal communication such as eye contact and gesture, making and keeping relationships) plus at least two kinds of restricted, repetitive behavior: stereotyped movements or speech (hand flapping, echolalia), insistence on sameness or rigid routines (including eating only a narrow set of the same foods), narrow interests of unusual intensity, and over- or under-reaction to sensory input. Symptoms begin in early development and impair function.
  • Premenstrual dysphoric disorder needs at least five symptoms in the final week before menses that ease within a few days after menses starts and are minimal the week after. At least one must be a core mood symptom: marked lability, irritability or anger, depressed mood or hopelessness, or anxiety and tension. The rest come from a broader list: less interest in usual activities, poor concentration, fatigue, appetite change or cravings, sleeping too much or too little, feeling out of control or overwhelmed, and physical symptoms such as breast tenderness, bloating, weight gain or joint aches. Confirm the cycle-linked pattern with prospective daily ratings over at least two cycles, and rule out a premenstrual flare of another disorder.
  • Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness caused by a medical condition, a substance or withdrawal. Think in two layers. Predisposing factors make the brain vulnerable: older age, dementia or other cognitive impairment, earlier brain injury or stroke, sensory impairment, frailty, heavy alcohol use and serious medical illness. Precipitating factors trigger the episode: infection, surgery, metabolic or electrolyte disturbance, hypoxia, pain, and drugs such as sedatives, anticholinergics and opioids. Low-dose antipsychotics such as haloperidol are used short-term for severe agitation; they treat symptoms, not the cause.

Depression and its look-alikes

Presentation Defining feature
Major depressive disorder At least 5 symptoms (including depressed mood or anhedonia) for at least 2 weeks
MDD with atypical features Mood brightens with good news; increased appetite and sleep; heavy, leaden limbs; sensitivity to rejection
Peripartum onset Onset in pregnancy or within 4 weeks of delivery. Postpartum blues are mild, start within days and pass by 2 weeks without impairment. Postpartum psychosis is rare but an emergency (risk of infanticide) and needs hospitalization
Seasonal pattern Episodes begin and remit at the same time of year for at least 2 years
Persistent depressive disorder Sub-threshold depressed mood for at least 2 years (1 year in children and adolescents). PDD plus a major depressive episode is "double depression"
Cyclothymic disorder At least 2 years of hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full criteria for an episode
Bereavement Not a disorder. Sadness comes in waves mixed with positive memories; self-esteem is intact; no suicidal plan, psychosis or marked slowing; function continues
Demoralization Not a disorder. Helplessness, hopelessness and loss of meaning after an illness
Substance- or medication-induced Starts during or soon after a substance (steroids, beta-blockers, illicit drugs) and resolves when it is stopped
Due to a medical condition For example multiple sclerosis, stroke, lupus or thyroid disease

Psychosis: more of the differential. Psychosis in mania tends to be grandiose, religious or sexual; in depression it is mood-congruent (guilt, sin, voices calling the person worthless). In bipolar II, psychosis can occur only during the depressive episodes. Substance-induced psychosis starts abruptly and clears soon after the substance is stopped. Delirium brings hallucinations with inattention and a fluctuating level of consciousness. Cluster A personality disorders are lifelong and odd but do not include hallucinations or disorganized thinking. Persistent depressive disorder and cyclothymia never include psychosis.

Onset Abrupt, later Insidious, early

More mental status terms. Perseveration is returning to the same idea whatever is asked. Delusions are classed by theme: persecutory, grandiose, referential (things are about or meant for the person) and somatic. An illusion misreads a real stimulus; a hallucination has no stimulus. Depersonalization is feeling that one's self or body is unreal; derealization is feeling that the outside world is unreal. Typical mental status pictures: depression (unkempt, slowed, long pauses, quiet speech), mania (pressured speech, flight of ideas, clanging, expansive affect, grandiosity), anxiety (restlessness, tremor, hypervigilance, ruminations) and psychosis (disorganization, thought blocking, flat affect, delusions).

Anxiety, OCD-related and trauma disorders

Disorder Key criteria
Panic disorder Unexpected attacks that peak within minutes with at least 4 symptoms, followed by at least a month of worry about further attacks or a change in behavior; linked with mitral valve prolapse
Agoraphobia Fear or avoidance of at least 2 of: public transport, open spaces, enclosed spaces, lines or crowds, being outside the home alone. It can occur with or without panic disorder
OCD Ego-dystonic obsessions and compulsions taking more than an hour a day; associated with tic disorders
Hoarding Persistent difficulty discarding possessions, so living spaces become unusable
Body dysmorphic disorder Preoccupation with a flaw others cannot see or think slight, with repetitive checking or grooming
Trichotillomania, excoriation Recurrent hair pulling or skin picking despite attempts to stop
PTSD After a qualifying trauma: intrusion, avoidance, negative thoughts and mood, and arousal symptoms for more than 1 month
Acute stress disorder The same kind of picture lasting 3 days to 1 month, with at least 9 of 14 symptoms

Always consider medical and substance causes of anxiety: hyperthyroidism, arrhythmias, asthma, pheochromocytoma, caffeine or stimulants, steroids, and withdrawal from alcohol, sedatives or opioids.

Withdrawal syndromes

Substance Withdrawal picture
Alcohol, benzodiazepines Anxiety, tremor, sweating, tachycardia, hypertension, seizures, hallucinosis. The only withdrawals that can kill
Opioids Dilated pupils, yawning, tearing and runny nose, gooseflesh, muscle aches, cramps, diarrhea; unpleasant but rarely dangerous
Stimulants Fatigue, increased sleep and appetite, depressed mood, suicidal thinking (the crash)
Cannabis Insomnia, irritability, restlessness, low mood, aggression

Alcohol withdrawal follows a clock: tremor and anxiety within hours, seizures peaking at 24 to 48 hours, delirium tremens (confusion with autonomic surge) from 48 to 96 hours. Combining alcohol or opioids with benzodiazepines is the classic life-threatening intoxication, through respiratory depression.

Delirium: common triggers. Benzodiazepines, anticholinergic drugs (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine), opioids, muscle relaxants and steroids; infection (urinary infection in older adults), surgery, pain, poor sleep and immobility. Look for fluctuating alertness, disorientation, inattention (serial 7s, spelling WORLD backward) and visual hallucinations or picking at the air, in either a hyperactive or a quiet, hypoactive form.

Child and adolescent psychiatry. Get collateral from parents and teachers: children report internal states such as anxiety and sadness better, while adults report behavior and duration better, and some diagnoses, such as ADHD, need symptoms in more than one setting. Disorders to separate: disruptive mood dysregulation disorder (severe recurrent outbursts with irritable mood between them), oppositional defiant disorder (defiant toward authority but gets along with peers), conduct disorder (violates others' rights; linked to adult antisocial PD), ADHD, autism and reactive attachment disorder (after severe early neglect). Separation anxiety disorder and selective mutism are childhood diagnoses. Children with depression or anxiety often have physical complaints; anxious children may become oppositional to avoid a feared situation; bipolar disorder in youth looks more chronic and reactive; and young children with PTSD may re-enact the trauma in repetitive play.

Eating disorders. Anorexia nervosa: restriction and low body weight, sometimes with bingeing or purging. Bulimia nervosa: binges with loss of control plus compensation (vomiting, laxatives, exercise), usually at normal weight. Binge-eating disorder: binges without compensation. Pica: eating non-food substances; check for iron or zinc deficiency.

High-yield clues

Each clue names what to notice in a stem and what to work out from it.

  • Psychosis and mood together: ask what happens when the mood is normal. Psychosis that persists for weeks without mood symptoms changes the diagnosis.
  • An elevated mood episode: check duration, function, psychosis and hospitalization before choosing between hypomania and mania.
  • An isolated, odd or withdrawn adult: decide why they are alone (no interest, fear of rejection, odd beliefs or distrust) to place the personality disorder.
  • An anxious patient who avoids places: name what is feared, and count the kinds of situations avoided.
  • A patient who is mute, rigid or holds odd postures: use the catatonia vocabulary precisely, and do not confuse catalepsy with cataplexy.
  • A young child with social differences: check both halves of autism (social communication and restricted, repetitive behavior), and know examples of each.
  • Mood symptoms tied to the menstrual cycle: know the full PMDD symptom list and what it excludes.
  • Acute confusion in a medically ill patient: separate the vulnerable brain (predisposing factors) from the trigger (precipitating factors).
  • An intoxicated or withdrawing patient: use the pupils, the eye movements and the vital signs to name the substance.
  • A grieving or ill patient who is sad: decide whether it is bereavement, demoralization or a depressive disorder.

2. Psychiatric management, suicide and psychopharmacology (10–11 questions)

These items ask what to do first, which therapy or drug fits this patient, and what to monitor. Read the stem for the detail that rules options out: a comorbidity, a pregnancy, a safety concern or a side effect.

First steps and evidence-based psychotherapy

Situation First priority or best-evidence approach Why the alternatives fall short
Borderline personality disorder with self-harm Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) Only therapy with strong evidence for fewer suicide attempts and admissions in this group
Underweight patient with an eating disorder Medical stabilization and weight restoration, watching for refeeding syndrome Therapy and drugs work poorly in a starved brain
Bulimia nervosa, binge-eating disorder CBT; fluoxetine is FDA-approved for bulimia
Major depression (mild to moderate) CBT, interpersonal therapy, behavioral activation, or an SSRI Psychoanalysis lacks evidence as first-line
Panic disorder, specific phobia, agoraphobia, OCD Exposure-based CBT (exposure and response prevention for OCD)
PTSD Trauma-focused therapy (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy)

Suicide: risk, theory and discharge planning

  • Interpersonal theory of suicide (Joiner). Death by suicide requires three conditions together: thwarted belongingness, perceived burdensomeness and an acquired capability for lethal self-injury. Keep this separate from physician-specific warning signs and from the "compulsive triad" (guilt, self-doubt, exaggerated responsibility) described in physicians.
  • Safety planning is done with anyone who has suicidal thoughts, a recent attempt, or is leaving a psychiatric unit. It covers warning signs, internal coping strategies, people and places for distraction, people to ask for help, professional contacts, and reducing access to lethal means.
  • No-harm contracts do not prevent suicide. They are not a substitute for a safety plan.
  • Risk rises in the weeks after psychiatric discharge, so planning before discharge matters even when the patient feels better.

Antipsychotics: potency predicts side effects

Antidepressants: matching the drug to the patient

  • Contraindications that steer the choice. Bupropion lowers the seizure threshold, so avoid it in bulimia with purging or anorexia (electrolyte shifts). TCAs are dangerous in overdose and with electrolyte or cardiac problems. MAOIs require dietary and drug interaction precautions.
  • Half-life matters. Fluoxetine's long half-life makes missed doses and discontinuation forgiving; paroxetine and venlafaxine cause the worst discontinuation symptoms.
  • Side-effect matching. Mirtazapine for poor appetite and insomnia; bupropion when sexual side effects or weight gain are a concern; SNRIs or TCAs when neuropathic pain coexists.
  • Pregnancy and breastfeeding. Untreated depression carries real risks (preterm birth, low birth weight, pre-eclampsia, poor bonding). Medication risks are small and come mainly from observational studies. The right conversation weighs both and is shared decision-making; many patients continue SSRIs in pregnancy and while breastfeeding.

Tics and Tourette syndrome

Tourette syndrome needs multiple motor tics and at least one vocal tic for more than a year, starting before age 18. Behavioral therapy (comprehensive behavioral intervention for tics) is first-line. Alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) are first-line drugs, especially with ADHD; antipsychotics such as aripiprazole are used for severe tics. Stimulants and atomoxetine treat ADHD but can worsen tics in some patients.

Depression treatment details. Duloxetine (an SNRI) treats depression and chronic pain together. If an SNRI causes intolerable side effects, switch to an SSRI rather than another SNRI. Bupropion has fewer sexual side effects. Venlafaxine raises blood pressure at higher doses. Trazodone can cause priapism, a medical emergency. Treatment-resistant depression means at least two adequate antidepressant trials have failed. Options include augmentation (lithium, an antipsychotic), MAOIs, ECT, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) and ketamine or esketamine.

Option Key facts
ECT Most effective treatment for severe or resistant depression; also for bipolar depression, catatonia and resistant psychosis. Main side effect is short-term memory loss. Pregnancy is not a contraindication; relative contraindications include a brain mass, unstable aneurysm and severe heart disease
rTMS Better tolerated than ECT, with no anesthesia or memory effects, but less effective and needs daily sessions; avoid with seizure risk or implanted metal or devices
Ketamine, esketamine NMDA receptor antagonists with a rapid antidepressant effect. Monitor during dosing for dissociation, hallucinations, raised blood pressure and nausea. Esketamine is given with an oral antidepressant

Bipolar treatment. Acute mania: lithium, valproate or an antipsychotic. Maintenance: lithium. Bipolar depression: lamotrigine, quetiapine or lurasidone. Lamotrigine does not treat acute mania. An antidepressant alone can trigger mania. If lithium causes nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, switch to a second-generation antipsychotic such as olanzapine. In pregnancy, avoid valproate; lamotrigine is often preferred for maintenance, and lithium can be used with monitoring (small absolute risk of Ebstein anomaly).

  • Second-generation antipsychotics are first-line for schizophrenia because they cause fewer extrapyramidal effects.
  • Risperidone raises prolactin; switching to aripiprazole, a partial D2 agonist, lowers it. Aripiprazole commonly causes akathisia.
  • Olanzapine has the worst metabolic effects. Quetiapine causes the fewest extrapyramidal effects, so it is used for psychosis in Parkinson disease. Ziprasidone must be taken with food.
  • Timing of movement effects: acute dystonia within hours to days (benztropine or diphenhydramine), akathisia within days to weeks (propranolol), tardive dyskinesia after months to years (VMAT2 inhibitors such as valbenazine or deutetrabenazine).
  • Serotonin syndrome can follow a drug interaction as well as a new serotonergic drug, for example an antibiotic that blocks SSRI metabolism. Treat with stopping the drugs, IV fluids, benzodiazepines and cyproheptadine.

Child, adolescent and perinatal prescribing. Stimulants (methylphenidate, amphetamine) are first-line for ADHD; atomoxetine, a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor, is the nonstimulant option. Alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) treat tics, especially with ADHD. Antipsychotics are used for irritability in autism and for severe tics. Antidepressants, antiseizure drugs used as mood stabilizers and atomoxetine carry a warning for suicidal thinking in young people. Stimulants can reduce appetite and growth and cause insomnia or tics. Perinatal depression is the most common complication of pregnancy (10 to 15%) and responds to the usual treatments.

Eating disorder treatment. Anorexia: weight restoration and family-based therapy. Bulimia: CBT and fluoxetine; avoid bupropion. Binge-eating disorder: CBT, SSRIs or lisdexamfetamine. Pica: treat any deficiency.

Borderline PD medication. Psychotherapy is the treatment of choice. SSRIs can help impulsivity, and mood stabilizers or antipsychotics can help mood swings, anger and chronic suicidal thinking.

Suicide risk. A previous attempt is the strongest risk factor. Others (SAD PERSONS): male sex, age over 60 or under 30, depression, alcohol or drugs, loss of rational thinking, family history, an organized plan with access to means, no support, chronic illness or pain. Further risks include recent psychiatric discharge, trauma, military service, borderline PD, impulsivity, unemployment, homelessness, hopelessness and command hallucinations. Protective factors include strong supports, children in the home, pregnancy, religious or cultural beliefs against suicide, a sense of purpose, future plans and a good alliance with clinicians. When acute risk is high, inpatient admission restricts access to lethal means.

Physician health. Alcohol is the substance physicians most often misuse, followed by opioids. Physicians die by suicide more often than the public, although their rates of depression are similar, and suicide is a leading cause of death in medical students.

High-yield clues

  • Chronic self-harm with unstable relationships: know which therapy has the best evidence for reducing suicide attempts.
  • A severely underweight patient: decide what must happen before therapy or medication can work, and what to watch for while it happens.
  • A patient leaving hospital after an attempt: know what a safety plan contains, and what does not protect the patient.
  • A question about why people die by suicide: know the three conditions of the interpersonal theory, and keep them apart from the compulsive triad and physician-specific risks.
  • A child with tics: check for coexisting ADHD before choosing a drug.
  • Fever and stiffness on a new drug: decide whether it is NMS or serotonin syndrome from the drug, the tempo and the reflexes, then pick the test or treatment.
  • A patient on a mood stabilizer: match each drug to its monitoring labs and pregnancy risk.
  • A pregnant patient on an antidepressant: weigh the drug's risks against the risks of untreated illness.
  • A depressed patient with another condition: let the comorbidity (pain, eating disorder, seizures, missed doses, sexual side effects) choose the antidepressant.
  • An antipsychotic side-effect pattern: sedation, orthostasis and anticholinergic effects suggest low potency; dystonia and parkinsonism suggest high potency.
  • An alcohol or opioid use disorder: check the liver, the kidneys and current opioid use before choosing a relapse-prevention drug.

Multiple sclerosis

MS is autoimmune demyelination of the central nervous system: T cells and macrophages attack myelin and oligodendrocytes. The diagnosis rests on lesions separated in time and in space. Typical clues are optic neuritis, internuclear ophthalmoplegia (especially bilateral, in a young adult), a sensory level or band, Lhermitte sign and Uhthoff phenomenon. MRI shows periventricular, juxtacortical, infratentorial and cord lesions (enhancing when active); CSF shows oligoclonal bands.

Same concept from different angles. Demyelination is tested on both sides of the CNS/PNS border. Contrast an immune attack on central myelin (relapsing, hyperreflexia, oligoclonal bands) with one on peripheral myelin (acute ascending weakness, areflexia, high CSF protein), and with diseases of the neuron or junction instead of the myelin.

High-yield clues

  • Pain and temperature lost on both sides in a band, with vibration spared: think about the fibers crossing in the center of the cord.
  • Unsteady gait that worsens with eyes closed, with lost reflexes: think about the dorsal roots and dorsal columns, not the cerebellum.
  • Weakness on one side, pain and temperature loss on the other: work out the side and level from which tracts have crossed.
  • Dizziness on standing with other autonomic failure: trace the sympathetic pathway, including the lateral horn from T1 to L2.
  • Rapidly ascending weakness with lost reflexes after an infection: decide whether the attack is on peripheral myelin, the axon or the junction, and watch the breathing.
  • Sudden severe hypertension in someone with a high cord injury: know where the sympathetic surge comes from and why the brain cannot stop it.
  • A teenager with ataxia, lost reflexes and upgoing toes: think about what kills these patients, not just what weakens them.
  • Foot drop: test inversion and the ankle reflex to separate a nerve lesion from a root lesion.
  • Flaccid weakness with no sensory loss after a febrile illness: predict the reflexes from the cell that is lost.
  • Relapsing deficits in different places in a young adult: know both the diagnosis and the immune mechanism behind it.
  • Weakness that improves with effort, or worsens with it: place the problem before or after the synapse.

High-yield clues

  • A pupil light test: shine the light in each eye in turn and ask which pupil fails, and when. That separates the afferent limb from the efferent limb.
  • Facial signs on one side and body signs on the other: the cranial nerve sets the level; long-tract signs tell you medial or lateral.
  • Hoarseness, trouble swallowing, vertigo and a small pupil after a stroke: name the structure behind each sign and the artery that supplies them.
  • An eye that cannot adduct on side gaze but converges normally: localize to the pathway linking the two eyes, and predict which movements fail and which are spared.
  • A third nerve palsy with weakness of the opposite body: place the lesion by level and work out the vessel.
  • A small pupil with drooping eyelid: find the level of the sympathetic lesion from the signs that come with it (brainstem, lung apex, neck).
  • Vertical double vision that is worst going downstairs: use the head-tilt test to name the nerve and side.
  • Several cranial nerves out together with no long-tract signs: think about where those nerves travel side by side through the skull.

Ischemia: TIA, stroke subtypes and prevention

  • Transient ischemic attack is a focal deficit from ischemia with no infarct on imaging, usually lasting minutes to an hour. Treat it as a warning: the stroke risk is highest in the next days.

7. Movement disorders, cerebellum and dementia (6 questions)

These items ask you to name a movement disorder or dementia from its pattern, place it in the circuit, and predict its course. Age of onset, the first symptom and family history do most of the work.

Basal ganglia circuit in one paragraph

Dopamine from the substantia nigra pars compacta travels along nigrostriatal axons to terminals in the striatum (caudate and putamen). There it excites the direct pathway (D1, which releases movement) and inhibits the indirect pathway (D2, which brakes movement). Too little dopamine means too much braking: parkinsonism. Loss of striatal medium spiny neurons in the indirect pathway, or damage to the subthalamic nucleus, removes the brake: chorea or ballism.

Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines are positive allosteric modulators of GABA-A receptors (they increase opening frequency). Half-life shapes misuse and withdrawal. Shorter-acting drugs (triazolam, alprazolam, lorazepam, oxazepam, midazolam) work fast and carry more misuse risk; when they are stopped, withdrawal and rebound symptoms come sooner and harder. Longer-acting drugs (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide, clonazepam) leave the body slowly, so they taper themselves and are used to treat alcohol withdrawal. Withdrawal is like alcohol withdrawal: anxiety, tremor, tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, seizures. Flumazenil is the antagonist used in overdose, and it can precipitate seizures in dependent patients.

High-yield clues

  • A toxin or drug that blocks a synthetic enzyme: know which transmitters each enzyme makes, and which share a pathway.
  • Anxiety that rebounds soon after stopping a sedative: use half-life to pick the drug.
  • A bleed on an anticoagulant: know which factors are affected and how to restore them.
  • Stroke prevention: let the cause of the stroke (heart rhythm vs. artery disease) choose between anticoagulant and antiplatelet, and know each drug's mechanism.
  • A progressive motor neuron disease: know the drug that modestly prolongs survival and how it works.
  • Chorea: know which drug class reduces it and why dopamine-boosting drugs are wrong.
  • A new seizure disorder in an older adult: weigh tolerability and drug interactions when choosing.
  • An acute attack of a primary headache: know which headache type responds to oxygen and which to a triptan.
  • A seizure that will not stop: know the first drug and the order of what follows.

Five question formats

Every question on this exam fits one of five formats. Knowing which one you are facing tells you what to look for in the stem.

Format Approximate share The stem gives you It asks for Your reasoning move
1. Where is it? About 1 in 4 A deficit, a seizure pattern or an image Structure, level, tract or vessel Level from the cranial nerve, side from the crossing, vessel from the territory
2. What is it? About 1 in 5 A patient, image or specimen The diagnosis Find the one feature that rules out the look-alikes
3. What's the next step? About 1 in 5 A diagnosis you must first work out A drug, therapy, test or safety step Name the condition, then filter options by comorbidity, risk and evidence
4. What's the mechanism? About 1 in 6 A disease, drug effect or risk Pathophysiology, cause or theory Explain why, not just what
5. Given this, what else is true? About 1 in 6 A diagnosis you must work out An associated finding, complication or course Name it, then recall what travels with it

Formats 1 and 5 are mirror images: one goes from the findings to the lesion, the other from the lesion to the findings. Many items also include an image, so practice reading CT, MRI, gross specimens and micrographs.

Worked illustrations (new scenarios, not exam items)

1. Where is it? A 14-year-old has headaches and cannot look up. His pupils constrict when he focuses on a near object but barely react to light. Reasoning: vertical (upward) gaze and the pretectal light reflex are both organized in the rostral dorsal midbrain, while the near response uses a separate, more ventral route. Answer: the dorsal midbrain at the level of the superior colliculus and pretectum, often compressed by a pineal region tumor.

2. What is it? A 34-year-old has had a fixed belief for eight months that a coworker is secretly in love with him. He works full time, his speech is organized and he has no hallucinations or mood episodes. Reasoning: a non-bizarre delusion lasting more than a month, with function preserved and no other psychotic symptoms. Answer: delusional disorder (erotomanic type).

3. What's the next step? A 29-year-old with treatment-resistant schizophrenia is starting clozapine. Reasoning: clozapine's most dangerous early risk is agranulocytosis. Answer: monitor the absolute neutrophil count on a fixed schedule before and during treatment.

4. What's the mechanism? A 41-year-old has drooping eyelids and double vision that worsen through the day and improve after rest. Reflexes and sensation are normal. Reasoning: fatigable weakness of eye and bulbar muscles with normal reflexes points to the neuromuscular junction. Answer: antibodies against postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors.

5. Given this, what else is true? A knife wound severs the left half of the spinal cord at T10. Reasoning: the dorsal columns and corticospinal tract are uncrossed at this level; spinothalamic fibers have already crossed. Answer: left leg weakness and loss of vibration and position below T10, with loss of pain and temperature on the right starting a level or two below.

Integrated study approach

The final rewards connecting systems rather than memorizing them separately. A few connections run through the whole block:

  • Psychiatry meets neurology. Psychiatric symptoms are often the first sign of a neurological disease (movement disorders, frontotemporal dementia, copper or vitamin disorders, encephalitis). Psychiatric drugs cause neurological emergencies (rigidity and fever, extrapyramidal signs, seizures).
  • Pharmacology follows anatomy. Dopamine pathways explain both Parkinson treatment and antipsychotic side effects. GABA and glutamate explain sedatives, withdrawal and several antiseizure and ALS drugs.
  • Development and teratogens connect to epilepsy and mood care. Several antiseizure mood stabilizers carry neural tube risk, so folate and drug choice matter in people who could become pregnant.
  • Autonomic pathways appear across groups: Horner syndrome, orthostatic hypotension from central or spinal sympathetic loss, autonomic dysreflexia and the autonomic signs of some headaches.

High-yield patterns for exam day

  1. A cranial nerve sign on one side and body signs on the other side means the brainstem; the cranial nerve tells you the level.
  2. Bilateral, segmental pain and temperature loss with vibration spared means the crossing fibers in the center of the cord.
  3. Loss of reflexes points to the peripheral nervous system (root, nerve, anterior horn); brisk reflexes point to the CNS.
  4. Psychosis that persists after mood normalizes, or that appears without mood episodes, is not a pure mood disorder.
  5. Hypomania never includes psychosis or loss of function; if either is present, it is mania.
  6. In management questions, stabilize first (weight, safety, reversal of anticoagulation), then treat.
  7. In drug questions, look for the comorbidity or pregnancy that rules options out, and match the side effect to the drug class.
  8. In image questions, decide first: inside or outside the brain, bleed or mass, acute or old, which territory.

Layered probing questions

Work through each case step by step. Answers are indented under each step. All are new patients, not exam items.

1. Psychiatric diagnosis. A 21-year-old has had auditory hallucinations, disorganized speech and social withdrawal for three months, with no mood episodes and a negative drug screen.

  1. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    • Schizophreniform disorder (psychotic symptoms for 1 to less than 6 months).
  2. What would change the diagnosis to schizophrenia?
    • Symptoms (including prodromal or residual phases) lasting at least 6 months with functional decline.
  3. Which dopamine pathway is linked to the hallucinations, and which to negative symptoms?
    • Mesolimbic excess for positive symptoms; reduced mesocortical activity for negative symptoms.
  4. She starts haloperidol and develops sustained neck twisting within two days. What is it and how is it treated?
    • Acute dystonia; give an anticholinergic (benztropine) or diphenhydramine.

2. Psychiatric management. A 45-year-old with bipolar I disorder stable on lithium is started on hydrochlorothiazide and ibuprofen. A week later she has coarse tremor, vomiting and confusion.

  1. What is the likely problem?
    • Lithium toxicity.
  2. Why did these drugs cause it?
    • Both reduce renal lithium clearance (thiazides by increasing proximal reabsorption with sodium; NSAIDs by reducing renal blood flow).
  3. What test confirms it?
    • A serum lithium level, plus kidney function and electrolytes.
  4. What long-term monitoring does lithium need?
    • Levels, kidney function, TSH and calcium.

3. Spinal cord. A 70-year-old wakes after aortic surgery with paralysis of both legs and loss of pain and temperature below T8. Vibration and position are normal.

  1. Which part of the cord is damaged?
    • The anterior two-thirds.
  2. Which vessel?
    • The anterior spinal artery (often after loss of flow through the artery of Adamkiewicz).
  3. Why are vibration and position spared?
    • The dorsal columns are supplied by the posterior spinal arteries.
  4. Will his legs be flaccid or spastic in a month?
    • Spastic with brisk reflexes and Babinski signs, after an early period of spinal shock.

4. Brainstem. A 60-year-old has the right side of her tongue wasting and deviating to the right, with left arm and leg weakness and loss of vibration on the left.

  1. Where is the lesion?
    • Right medial medulla.
  2. Which structures explain each sign?
    • CN XII nucleus or fibers (tongue), pyramid (contralateral weakness), medial lemniscus (contralateral vibration loss).
  3. Which artery is most likely involved?
    • Branches of the anterior spinal or vertebral artery.
  4. Why is facial sensation normal?
    • The spinal trigeminal tract and nucleus lie laterally and are spared.

5. Stroke. A 72-year-old has sudden weakness and numbness of the right leg, with the arm and face much less affected, and seems apathetic.

  1. Which vessel is occluded?
    • Left anterior cerebral artery.
  2. Why is the leg worse than the arm?
    • The leg area of motor and sensory cortex lies on the medial surface of the hemisphere.
  3. What other sign might appear?
    • Urinary incontinence; abulia from medial frontal damage.
  4. Her ECG shows atrial fibrillation. What prevents another stroke?
    • Anticoagulation.

6. Cortex and language. A 68-year-old speaks fluently but with made-up words, cannot follow commands and does not seem aware of the problem.

  1. What type of aphasia is this?
    • Wernicke (receptive) aphasia.
  2. Where is the lesion?
    • Left posterior superior temporal gyrus.
  3. Which vessel?
    • Inferior division of the left MCA.
  4. What visual field defect might go with it?
    • Right upper quadrantanopia (Meyer loop in the temporal lobe).

7. Movement. A 74-year-old with diabetes and hypertension suddenly develops wild flinging movements of the left arm.

  1. What is the movement called?
    • Hemiballismus.
  2. Where is the lesion?
    • Right subthalamic nucleus.
  3. Why does that lesion cause excess movement?
    • The subthalamic nucleus drives the internal globus pallidus, which inhibits the thalamus; losing it releases thalamocortical activity.
  4. What is the likely cause?
    • A small-vessel (lacunar) stroke.

8. Headache. A 26-year-old woman with obesity has daily headaches worse lying down, brief episodes of blurred vision and horizontal double vision. MRI is normal.

  1. What is the most likely diagnosis?
    • Idiopathic intracranial hypertension.
  2. What will fundoscopy show?
    • Bilateral papilledema.
  3. Why the double vision?
    • Raised pressure stretches CN VI (a false localizing sign).
  4. What confirms it, and what treats it?
    • Lumbar puncture with high opening pressure and normal CSF; weight loss and acetazolamide.

9. Ventricles. A 78-year-old has a shuffling, magnetic gait, urinary incontinence and slowed thinking over a year.

  1. What is the likely diagnosis?
    • Normal pressure hydrocephalus.
  2. What will imaging show?
    • Enlarged ventricles out of proportion to atrophy.
  3. Is it communicating or obstructive?
    • Communicating (impaired CSF absorption).
  4. What test predicts benefit from a shunt?
    • Large-volume lumbar tap with gait assessment before and after.

10. Neuropathology. A 64-year-old has three months of headache and personality change. MRI shows a ring-enhancing mass crossing the corpus callosum.

  1. What is the likely tumor?
    • Glioblastoma.
  2. What will histology show?
    • Pseudopalisading necrosis and vascular proliferation.
  3. Which cell marker supports a glial origin?
    • GFAP.
  4. Why does the mass enhance?
    • Disruption of the blood-brain barrier lets gadolinium leak into the tumor.

11. Neuropharmacology. A 30-year-old with focal epilepsy starts phenytoin. Months later he has swollen gums and unsteady gait, and his level jumped sharply after a small dose increase.

  1. What explains the gums?
    • Gingival hyperplasia, a known phenytoin effect.
  2. What explains the gait?
    • Cerebellar toxicity (nystagmus and ataxia at high levels).
  3. Why did a small dose change raise the level so much?
    • Zero-order kinetics: its metabolism saturates.
  4. What is phenytoin's mechanism?
    • Blocks voltage-gated sodium channels, prolonging their inactive state.
